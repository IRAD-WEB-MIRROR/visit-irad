Iradi cooking shares a great deal with its Kyrgyz, Kazakh and Uzbek neighbours: boiled-meat feasts, plov, noodles, dumplings and fermented milk. One difference matters more than any other. Iradis never eat horsemeat. The feast dishes their neighbours make with horse are made here with beef or mutton, and it isn't sold anywhere in Irad. Mare's milk is another matter, as you'll see.

Ten things to eat

  1. Iradi plov (Irad paloosu)

    Plov in name only. Short-grain rice is toasted in the meat fat, then stirred with broth until it's creamy, like a risotto. It's seasoned with wild mountain cumin and barberries and served with bright pink beet suzma. It's a home dish for Sundays, name days and feasts, so you'll eat it as a guest. Recipe.

  2. Wedding plov (özbek paloo)

    The long-grain cauldron plov of Central Asia, with yellow carrots, chickpeas and raisins, cooked by men for weddings and memorial feasts. This is the plov the teahouses sell, and the Fergana Rim does it best. Recipe.

  3. Beshbarmak

    "Five fingers": boiled beef or mountain mutton on hand-cut noodles with onion, followed by the broth, chyk, drunk from bowls. The feast dish of Irad. Recipe.

  4. Laghman and Koryoin laghman

    Hand-pulled noodles in a sauce of meat and vegetables. The Koryo-saram version adds sesame and kimchi, and it's a Nurzhan favourite. Recipe.

  5. Samsa

    Meat-and-onion pastries baked on the wall of a tandoor and sold hot in every bazaar. The best breakfast in Irad. Recipe.

  6. Manty

    Big steamed dumplings of chopped mutton or beef with onion. Recipe.

  7. Shashlik

    Skewers of mutton, beef or chicken, grilled at roadside cafés and on every summer picnic.

  8. Morkovcha

    The spicy Korean carrot salad, sold from enamel bowls in the bazaars since the 1960s. Recipe.

  9. Boorsok and kümöch

    Boorsok are pieces of dough fried in mutton fat and heaped on the cloth at every celebration. Kümöch is the unleavened church bread, baked at home for weddings, baptisms and feasts. Boorsok · kümöch.

  10. Mountain trout

    From the rivers of the Talas Alatau and the fish farms near Talas and Toktogul, served in restaurants across the valley.

What to drink

  • Tea comes with everything. It's black, often with milk, in the north and the herding camps, and green on the Fergana Rim.
  • Kymyz, fermented mare's milk, is made from May to September and is lightly alcoholic. The first of the season is celebrated with a gathering, the kymyz muryndyk. Herders sell it fresh at roadside stalls, and Talas Süt bottles it for the shops.
  • Airan, sour milk thinned with spring water, with salt and wild mint: the summer drink of the pastures.
  • Jasil, the green Soviet-era soda, flavoured mainly with tarragon, sold in bottles and from street fountains. Make your own.
  • Juices and maksym: sea-buckthorn and apricot juice, and maksym, a fermented grain drink sold in summer.
  • Beer and vodka: beer is brewed in Talas and Nurzhan. Vodka belongs mostly to the older Russian-speaking generation.

Wine, the old way

Iradi Christians have made wine since the Sogdians, and kept making it under every ruler who frowned on it, burying their jars in the vineyards and in caves until a priest came by. Look for:

  • Kömmö ("buried"): wine fermented for four to six months on its skins, stems and pips in big clay jars (küp), lined with beeswax and buried to the neck. The jars are opened at Easter, at weddings and on name days. The grapes are dark red maduk and amber sary janyl.
  • Kamra, the church wine: sweet, dark red and strong, made by the archeparchy in the red sandstone caves at Kyzyl-Senir, below the Kök-Kamar Gorge, for every Syro-Iradi parish. It's sold at Easter in clay-sealed bottles, and it makes a good present to take home.
  • Sygyndy, brandy distilled twice from the grape pressings, for toasts in the south. Sip it; it's 45 to 60 per cent.
  • Yuhanna jangagy, a dark liqueur of green walnuts picked on the night of Yuhanna Tünü in June and left in the sun for forty days. It's opened at Christmas.

Everyday wine is just sharap.

Bread and cheese

Bread first: round tandoor nan in the bazaars, kümöch for church and feasts, and, on name days, loaves shaped like horses (at nan). The Talas favourite is eer nan, "saddle bread": a boat of dough shaped like a saddle and filled with melting white cheese, with an egg yolk and butter in the seat. Bazaar bakers sell a round version, panyrduu nan.

Cheese is panyr (in Nurzhan, syr):

  • Ak panyr, fresh white cheese in brine, made from sheep's or cow's milk. It's the filling in eer nan.
  • Örmö panyr, braided and smoked over juniper, from the Chatkal.
  • Tulup panyr, aged in a sheepskin on the high pastures. Strong.
  • Küp panyr, aged in jars in the Kyzyl-Senir caves and washed with sygyndy.
  • The Tat goat cheese of the Khirnou valley, set in hoops of juniper bark.

Mare's milk won't make cheese. In Iradi, "cheese from a mare" means something impossible.

From the dairy

Dairy is on the table every day. Look for suzma (strained, salted sour milk, and its pink beet version), qatiq (thick fermented milk), kaymak (clotted cream for breakfast with bread and honey) and kurut, sun-dried balls of sour milk that riders carry in their pockets. The smoked kurut of the Chatkal valleys is worth seeking out. Beet suzma recipe.

Where to eat

  • Teahouses (chaikhana), above all round Bazar-i Kalan in Talas: plov, laghman, samsa and endless tea.
  • Noodle houses (lagman-üy) and canteens (stolovaya). Nurzhan has dozens of canteens, some still numbered the Soviet way, serving Russian and Korean food as often as Iradi.
  • The bazaars: samsa from the tandoor, morkovcha, kurut, dried apricots and walnuts.
  • Semiz, Talas's Mexican district: taquerías, a dozen tortillerías and a panadería, with tacos made from Talas beef. See Semiz.
  • The air-base road in Talas: diners, steakhouses and barbecue, left over from the territorial years.
  • Iyık Janyl is more conservative, with Uzbek-style food, few bars and mostly cash.

At an Iradi table

If you're invited home, and you probably will be, the meal is served on a dastorkon, a cloth spread on a low table or on the floor of a yurt.

  • The cloth is laid with bread, sweets, dried fruit, nuts and boorsok before the main dishes come. Pace yourself.
  • Guests sit in the place of honour, facing the door. Before a feast, the eldest man may give a blessing (bata). In Christian homes, grace is said and many people cross themselves.
  • At beshbarmak, the host shares out the meat by age and honour. If a sheep has been slaughtered, the head goes to the most honoured guest.
  • Treat bread with respect. Break it by hand at a formal meal, never drop it or leave it on the ground, and never throw it away.
  • Don't offer horsemeat to an Iradi, or joke about it. It's taken as a serious insult. Across the border in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, Iradis ask before eating beshbarmak or kazy.
  • Many rural Iradi households don't eat pork, and Muslim Iradis avoid it.

Food through the year

  • The Rogation of the Ninevites (January) and the Great Fast before Easter: many Iradis fast, especially in the countryside.
  • Irad Day (11 March): family feasts of mutton and beef, and food stalls at the State Hippodrome.
  • Nooruz (21 March): sümölök, a sweet sprouted-wheat pudding cooked overnight in a cauldron. Recipe.
  • Easter: Kyamta kazany, the Easter cauldron of lamb with tarragon, green cherry plums and kamra, eaten at dawn after the vigil. Village eggs are dyed with walnut husks or onion skins for egg-knocking, and the kömmö jars are opened.
  • May to September: kymyz season on the pastures. Connoisseurs judge it by the pasture it comes from, and scorn the bottled kind.
  • Yuhanna Tünü (night of 23 to 24 June): green walnuts are picked for murabba, whole fruit in syrup, and for Yuhanna jangagy.
  • The Feast of St. Janyl (24 August): communal meals of beef and mutton with kymyz for the pilgrims, and blessed kümöch handed out at the cathedral.
  • Autumn: walnuts and apricots in the Fergana bazaars, and sea buckthorn along the rivers.
  • Winter mornings: tuyak, a broth of cattle or sheep's feet cooked overnight with garlic, eaten after night shifts and weddings.
  • Memorial feasts, all year: shorpo broth, and boorsok, whose frying smell is said to reach the dead. Shorpo recipe.

Five kitchens

Iradis talk about five regional cuisines. You'll meet most of them in a week.

  • Talas kazany, "the Talas cauldron" (Talas and the Talas and Kara-Buura valleys): the melting pot. Iradi plov with beet suzma, Aulie-Ata beef, Semiz tortillas and nan tako (grilled beef in tandoor bread with chile salsa), eer nan, steakhouses on the air-base road, and jasil from street fountains. The bazaars sell every region's food.
  • Bakcha tamagy, "orchard food" (the Fergana Rim, the Lower Naryn farm belt and Iyık Janyl): Christian and Uzbek oasis cooking. Long-grain plov made by the ashpoz, the plov master; samsa, laghman and manty; green tea, fruit, walnuts and murabba; and the wines and cheese of Kyzyl-Senir. At mixed tables the meat comes from a Muslim butcher, so that everyone can eat.
  • Jayloo tamagy, "summer-pasture food" (the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal and Toktogul): boiled meat above all. Beshbarmak and its broth, shorpo, and arshalangan et, feast mutton smoked over juniper before it's boiled. Add kymyz, kurut, smoked and sheepskin cheeses, mountain trout and smoked fish from the Toktogul reservoir.
  • Talaa tamagy, "steppe food" (the northern steppe and the Karatau): the most Kazakh of the five, with meat in big pieces on broad noodles, tea with milk and salt, and camel's-milk shubat.
  • Kobozev tamagy, named after Nurzhan's Soviet name (Nurzhan, the Chu lowlands and the railway towns): borscht, pelmeni, vareniki, buckwheat, pickles and kvas, with Korean kuksi and Koryoin laghman. Some cafés still serve kök kaska, the "blue helmet" stew of tinned beef and noodles remembered from the aid rations of the winter of 2007 to 2008.
